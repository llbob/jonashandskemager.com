<p>The dancing body is often understood as a liberated body, yet classical ballet had already developed a standardized repertoire of movements that disciplined and constrained it. With the Tiller Girls, Fordist machine logic took possession of this body, and the rationalization of work found its aesthetic expression: a new product of the culture industry was born. Disney’s animated feel-good universe perfected the interplay of rhythmic repetition, synchronicity, and seemingly mechanical movement, combining it with dancing and singing galore. Movement once again presented itself as an expression of freedom and imagination. For the proponents of ideology critique, however, it merely mirrored the industrially organized world: repeatable, predictable, consumable. For them, the body itself had become a choreographed object.</p><p><br><br>The couple in track pants dancing in Arne Jacobsen’s iconic room at the SAS Hotel seems to have stepped right out of <em>Snow White</em>, Disney’s first animated feature film, or the thicket of the <em>Jungle Book</em>. Somehow displaced in this walk-in time capsule, they challenge the iconic image of the modernist interior, but also point to their common origin: modernism, with its manifold attempts to aestheticize mass production and rationalization, embraced standardization just as much as it celebrated individuality of form. On the one hand, improved design promised a better quality of life for everyone; on the other, mass-produced happiness on celluloid shaped the very needs that people came to demand. Yet while Disney serves as a key example of a popular culture that seeks to orchestrate the masses, the design and architecture of Jacobsen and his contemporaries became relics of an era that is now desired, if not fetishized, for its aesthetic rather than its ideas. Forms migrate, ideologies shift, when your way of dancing collides with an authoritarian idea of beauty.</p><p><br><br></p><p>Interestingly, dance and furniture meet precisely where commodity fetishism has sought a concise metaphor for its illustration. It was Karl Marx who imagined furniture in playful, rhythmic motion. The moving table, animated by a ghostly hand, visualizes how commodities present themselves within capitalist society. As soon as the table is transformed from a functional object into a commodity, it becomes a “sensuously supersensible thing”: no longer standing with its feet on the ground, but on its head. Its movement seems mysterious, and yet it stems entirely from the very socio-economic conditions of the market that transformed it into a product to be offered, desired, and sold.</p><p><br><br></p><p>Peder Moos’ dining table, which fetched a record price at auction, is such an enchanted piece of furniture. It gets a cartoonish remake in the style of the Zagreb School of Animation, which was developed as an alternative to the images produced by Disney &amp; Co. With its aesthetic inspired by progressive socialist ideals, it must be aware of the connotations of its dancing feet. But this table, handmade and one of a kind, has entered territories where the fetishization of the commodity takes on a different meaning.</p><p><br><br></p><p>If you take metaphors literally, they tell of surreal encounters and manage to turn things upside down. Jonas Handskemager animates the past preserved in design, allowing form and aesthetics to reflect on their ideology. The encrusted is ready to move. If you understand how a lock works, any door can be opened. You could enter and turn up the music. “If you want to force the frozen circumstances to dance, you have to sing them their own melody.”</p><p><br><br></p><p><em>By Vanessa Joan Müller, </em>curator and writer</p><p><br><br></p><p>The exhibition is kindly supported by The Danish Arts Foundation and Københavns Kommune Rådet for Visuel Kunst</p><p><br><br></p><p>Thanks to Tristan Kold</p><p><br><br></p>
